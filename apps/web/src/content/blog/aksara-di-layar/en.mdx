Walk through Denpasar and nearly every office, school, and hotel sign has two lines: Balinese script on top, Latin letters below. The rule comes from Article 6 of Bali Governor Regulation No. 80 of 2018 on protecting and using the Balinese language, script, and literature.

The article covers sacred places, customary institutions, inauguration plaques, buildings, government and private institutions, street names, tourism facilities, and other public facilities. The Balinese script goes above the Latin letters, at a balanced size.

The Bali edition receptionist robots and Taksu Kiosk stand in exactly those places, so their screens and printouts follow the same layout.

## From Latin letters to Aksara Bali

Venue names and visitor names change all the time, so we cannot prepare an image of every word. The kiosk converts Latin text into Aksara Bali on the spot, using the spelling rules common today:

- consonant clusters are written with adeg-adeg, and the font stacks them into gantungan;
- ng, r, and h at the end of a syllable become cecek, surang, and bisah;
- numbers become Balinese digits.

One thing cannot be guessed from Latin letters: the sound of e. A plain e is read as pepet, as in Ketut. For the taling sound, as in Madé, type é. A short list of common names is read correctly without the accent.

## Where it stops

The transliteration follows rules and does not use a dictionary yet. Loanwords from Kawi or Sanskrit sometimes have their own spelling in the script that cannot be derived from their Latin form.

So for official signs, plaques, or anything printed to last, we review the result with a Balinese language adviser first. The kiosk says the same thing every time it shows a transliteration.
